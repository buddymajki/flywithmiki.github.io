How to choose a paragliding school in Switzerland

The 8 questions that actually matter, including the ones schools hope you will not ask. Take this list to every school you talk to, including us.

The 8 Questions ↓ Our Own Numbers →

Why would a school publish a guide like this?

Because the Swiss training market is genuinely hard to compare: every school prices differently, bundles differently and hides different things in the fine print. We spent weeks reconstructing the real, all-in costs of training across Central Switzerland from schools' own published numbers, and the result is on this page. If after reading it you choose another school with open eyes, this page has done its job. Most people who compare carefully end up somewhere they trust, and trust is the one thing we refuse to compete on price with.

Start with the one fact that changes everything

Once you know this, cheap-looking offers stop being confusing.

In Switzerland, the honest all-in total from zero to the licence, with new equipment, lands in roughly the same range almost everywhere: about CHF 9,000 to 12,000. That covers tuition, a complete new beginner kit, SHV fees, exams and cable cars. Headline course prices, on the other hand, range from about CHF 1,900 to 4,600, a difference of more than double.

How can both be true? Because the money moves between lines, not away. A school with a low headline price typically earns the difference on the equipment you are required to buy there, on surcharges if you bring your own wing (commonly CHF 1,000 to 2,000 in this region), or on extensions and extras. A school with a higher flat rate has usually just put more of the real total on the first line. Neither model is a scam; but only one of them lets you compare schools by reading their homepage. So never compare headline prices. Compare honest totals.

The 8 questions to ask every school

1 What is the real all-in total, in writing?

Tuition + complete equipment + SHV membership + insurance + two exams + cable cars. If a school cannot or will not put that number in writing, the number is not going to shrink later.

Ask: “What will I have spent, in total, on the day I hold my licence?”
Our answer: CHF 11,000 to 12,000, itemised line by line on our prices page. Nothing on top.

2 Is the course price tied to buying your equipment there?

Very common in this region: the advertised price only applies if you buy your complete kit at the school, and bringing your own gear costs CHF 1,000 to 2,000 extra, sometimes mentioned only in an FAQ. The tie itself is a legitimate business model (schools live partly from equipment sales, and a wing your instructor chose and checked is a real safety benefit); a tie that is not printed clearly is the problem.

Ask: “What exactly does the course cost if I do NOT buy my wing here, and what do I get for the difference?”
Our answer: both prices in the open, side by side: CHF 4,500 with our equipment package, CHF 6,300 with your own gear, which includes a 30-minute airworthiness review of your kit (a full check and reserve repack are a separate, clearly priced service if the review calls for them). Details →

3 How many students per instructor, really?

At the launch and in the landing field, an instructor can only truly watch one student at a time. Everything about your progress speed and your safety margin follows from this number. Websites say “small groups”; days on the hill say otherwise. Also ask how many new students per year the school takes: that is the honest capacity number.

SIZE MATTERS. Here is the math nobody shows you:

With beginners, an instructor can safely guide one student in the air at a time. Everyone else waits at launch until that student has landed, or stands there unsupervised. At a big school with 15+ students on the hill, it is completely normal to stand at launch, harness on, ready, and wait 30 to 60 minutes before you are allowed to take off. At a small school, that queue is a few minutes. And on our themed days Miki does not watch from the landing field with binoculars: he flies with the group.

The same math decides how much you actually fly. On a normal high-flight or maneuver-training day, our students typically log 4 to 6 flights; in a large group, 2 to 3 is the realistic norm, simply because everyone shares the same instructor and the same clock. So a course that looks a few hundred francs cheaper can quietly deliver half the flights and a quarter of the instructor's attention. Per flight, and per minute of someone actually watching you, the small school is usually the cheapest offer on the market. In training, that attention is not comfort: it is exactly what you are paying for.

Ask: “On a normal Saturday in season, how many students share one instructor? And how many new students do you take per year?”
Our answer: 3-8 students per instructor, hard limit, and deliberately only about 20 new students per year. When we are full, we say so.

4 Will you understand your instructor at 800 meters?

You will have a radio in your ear during every flight, and the instructions coming through it matter most exactly when you are stressed. If you are not fully comfortable in Swiss German, this is not a “nice to have”: full English (or your language) instruction in the air is rare in Central Switzerland, and worth asking about precisely.

Ask: “Is the radio guidance during flights available in my language, or just the theory lessons?”
Our answer: most of our students speak English fluently and ground briefings normally run in English; in the air you get the radio instructions in English, German or Hungarian, whichever you react fastest in.

5 How old is the training gear, and what radio will be in your ear?

Almost nobody asks this one, and it is a genuine look under the hood. Your very first days on the training hill are the most formative of your whole flying life, and they are exactly where equipment quality matters most. A modern school wing inflates willingly and rewards you for doing things right; a worn-out, end-of-life wing fights you on every launch, and a beginner has no way of knowing whether the problem is the wing or themselves. Many quietly conclude it is themselves. The radio is the same story: your instructor's voice is your lifeline in the air, and it should arrive through a robust radio built for flying, loud and clear through a headset, not through a bargain handset that crackles or drops out at the moment you need it most.

Ask: “How old are the school wings I will learn on at the training hill? And what radios do you use for guidance in the air?”
Our answer: you train on modern, well-maintained school wings that inflate easily and actually fly, because the first steps decide whether you fall in love with this sport. In the air you hear us through good-quality PMR radios with headsets, the same setup we recommend you buy for yourself (about CHF 200 plus CHF 29 for the headset). Come and inspect the gear before you sign up; we like this question.

6 Who exactly will teach you, and can you verify it?

Every certified Swiss instructor has a public entry in the SHV/FSVL registry. A school that is proud of its instructors will give you names and SHV numbers without hesitation. At bigger schools, also ask who teaches you day to day; it is often not the person on the homepage.

Ask: “What is the name and SHV number of the instructor who will actually train me? Where can I look it up?”
Our answer: Miki Mohos, SHV/BAZL instructor #54547, verify in the official SHV registry. 950+ flights, 435+ hours, XC records flown on EN-A wings, the same class you will learn on, verifiable on XContest. And you do not have to take a website's word for the fit: before you enrol, every prospective student has a free 30-to-60-minute video call with Miki himself, not with an office. If the chemistry is not right, better to find out before you pay.

7 What happens when life interferes?

Most people learn alongside a job, and Swiss weather does not read your calendar. The expensive surprises live here: courses that expire after fixed months, per-flight fees that punish a rained-out season, paid extensions, weekday-only conditions. Read the validity and extension rules before you compare anything else.

Ask: “What does it cost if I need longer than planned? What exactly happens to my money if I have to pause?”
Our answer: unlimited high flights, 12 months from your first flight, and if you genuinely need more time we talk and find a solution; we do not invoice it.

8 What do former students say, where you can check it?

Not testimonials picked by the school: public, dated reviews under real names, on Google or elsewhere. Read the middling ones, not just the best; recurring themes (crowding, waiting, communication) are more informative than star averages. And if a school shows no reviews anywhere, ask yourself why.

Ask: nothing. Just search the school's name on Google Maps and read.
Our answer: 4.9 from 47 public Google reviews, read them all here, the critical ones included, if you find any.

Where we are NOT the right choice, honestly

If you want a large school with a big shop, many instructors and courses starting every week, we are not that: we are one certified instructor with a coaching team and about 20 new students per year. If you want the absolute lowest all-in total and are happy hunting for it, you can find paths a few hundred francs cheaper in Switzerland. And if you need everything in Swiss German with a big local club culture around it, a bigger traditional school may fit you better. We compete on attention per student, transparency and verifiable credentials, not on being everything for everyone.

One more thing, whichever school you choose

Learning to fly is an intimate thing. In your first days you trust your instructor the way a child trusts the hand holding the bicycle seat, and in the air you do not really meet the wind: you meet your own limits. Whether that becomes the most beautiful chapter of your life or a story of quiet frustration depends less on the price list than on the person standing next to you. Almost every pilot remembers their first instructor for the rest of their life. Choose the one whose voice you will want to hear in your head at 2,000 meters, thirty years from now. Everything else on this page is secondary to that.
